## Archetype Proposal: The Decision-Making Cycle
### 1. Context-template
A [DECISION] must be made by a [DECISION-MAKER] based on [INFORMATION], and once a [DECISION] is taken, it cannot be easily reversed. As [INFORMATION] accumulates, the [DECISION-MAKER] learns that earlier [DECISION]s may no longer be optimal. The [REVERSAL_COST] increases with the number of prior [DECISION]s, often leading the [DECISION-MAKER] to stick with initial choices even when new [INFORMATION] suggests a different course. [DECISION_THEORY] explores how rational [DECISION-MAKER]s balance the value of additional [INFORMATION] against the [REVERSAL_COST].

### 3. Parallel contexts

**Business Strategy**

*Form (a):*  
A business executive must choose a plan based on market data, and once a plan is decided upon, it cannot be easily reversed. As market data accumulates, the executive learns that earlier plans may no longer be optimal. Sunk costs grow with the number of prior plans, often leading the executive to stick with initial choices even when new data suggests a different course. Decision theory explores how rational executives balance the value of additional market data against the cost of changing plans.

*Form (b):*  
A company’s executive must select a strategic plan based on current market data. Once the plan is set, reversing it is costly. As new market information arrives, it may indicate that earlier strategies are suboptimal. Yet, due to sunk costs, executives often continue with their original plan, even when evidence favors a different approach. Decision theory examines how rational decision-makers weigh the benefits of new information against the costs of changing course.

**Military Command**

*Form (a):*  
A general must decide on an attack plan based on intelligence, and once the attack is launched, it cannot be easily reversed. As intelligence updates arrive, the general learns that earlier attack plans may be suboptimal. The strategic retreat cost increases with the number of prior attack plans, often causing the general to persist with initial plans even when new intelligence suggests a different move. Command theory studies how rational generals balance the value of intelligence against the cost of changing plans.

*Form (b):*  
A general must choose an attack strategy based on intelligence reports. Once the attack is underway, reversing the plan is costly. As more intelligence comes in, it may show that earlier attack plans are no longer optimal. However, due to strategic retreat costs, generals tend to stick with their original plans, even when new intelligence indicates a better option. Command theory analyzes how rational military leaders decide when to adapt their plans.

**Personal Life**

*Form (a):*  
An individual must choose a life path based on advice, and once a decision is made, it cannot be easily reversed. As advice accumulates, the individual learns that earlier choices may be suboptimal. Regret costs grow with the number of prior choices, often leading the individual to stick with initial decisions even when new advice suggests a different direction. Decision analysis explores how rational individuals weigh the value of new advice against the regret of changing course.

*Form (b):*  
A person must decide on a life path based on advice received. Once the decision is made, reversing it is costly in terms of regret. As more advice is gathered, it may indicate that earlier choices were not ideal. Yet, due to regret costs, people often persist with their original decisions, even when current advice favors a different path. Decision analysis examines how rational individuals balance the benefits of new advice against the costs of changing their decisions.

**Scientific Research**

*Form (a):*  
A scientist must formulate a hypothesis based on experimental data, and once a hypothesis is proposed, it cannot be easily reversed. As more data is collected, the scientist learns that earlier hypotheses may be less valid. The cost of experimental rework increases with the number of prior hypotheses, often leading the scientist to stick with initial hypotheses even when new data suggests alternatives. Scientific method studies how rational scientists weigh the value of additional data against the cost of revising hypotheses.

*Form (b):*  
A researcher must develop a hypothesis based on experimental data. Once the hypothesis is set, changing it is costly. As further data is gathered, it may challenge earlier hypotheses. Nonetheless, due to the costs of reworking experiments, scientists tend to stick with their initial hypotheses even when new data indicates better explanations. Scientific methodology explores how rational scientists decide when to revise hypotheses.

**Political Campaign**

*Form (a):*  
A campaign strategist must adopt a policy based on polling data, and once a policy is adopted, it cannot be easily reversed. As polling data updates, the strategist learns that earlier policies may be less popular. The reputational cost of changing policies increases with the number of prior policies, often causing the strategist to stick with initial policies even when new polling suggests a different approach. Political science studies how rational strategists balance the value of new polling data against the reputational costs of policy shifts.

*Form (b):*  
A campaign team must decide on a policy based on polling data. Once the policy is announced, reversing it is costly in terms of reputation. As polling data evolves, it may favor a different policy. Yet, due to reputational costs, strategists often maintain their original policies, even when current polling indicates a better alternative. Political science examines how rational campaign teams weigh the benefits of adapting policies against reputational risks.

## 4. Justification
This archetype captures the universal dilemma faced by decision-makers under uncertainty, where past commitments influence current choices, and the cost of reversal shapes behavior across domains as diverse as business, military, personal life, science, and politics.

---

## 5. Notes
- The template is intentionally broad to encompass various decision contexts.
- The metanyms are chosen to reflect the core elements: decision, decision-maker, information, reversal cost, and path.
- The parallel contexts demonstrate the archetype's flexibility and the structural similarity despite domain differences.
- The recursive nature is evident in the personal life context, where individual choices mirror organizational decisions, but the archetype itself is not strictly recursive.

## Archetype Proposal: The Resource Allocation Dilemma

A [RESOURCE] must be allocated by a [ALLOCATOR] among competing [TARGETS], and once a [ALLOCATION] is made, it cannot be easily retracted. As [NEED] signals emerge, the [ALLOCATOR] learns that earlier [ALLOCATION]s may no longer be optimal. The [RETRACTION_COST] increases with the number of prior [ALLOCATION]s, often leading the [ALLOCATOR] to persist with initial distributions even when new [NEED] signals suggest a different allocation. [OPTIMIZATION] studies how rational [ALLOCATOR]s balance the value of additional [NEED] information against the [RETRACTION_COST].

### 3. Parallel contexts

**Government Budgeting**

*Form (a):*  
A government must allocate funds among departments, and once a budget is allocated, it cannot be easily retracted. As demand signals emerge, the government learns that earlier allocations may no longer be optimal. The opportunity cost of reallocation increases with the number of prior allocations, often leading to persistence with initial budgets even when new demand signals suggest a different distribution. Optimization studies how rational policymakers balance the value of additional demand information against the opportunity cost of reallocating funds.

*Form (b):*  
A government allocates funds to various departments. Once the funds are distributed, reversing allocations is costly. As new demand signals come in, they may indicate that earlier allocations are no longer appropriate. However, due to opportunity costs, policymakers tend to stick with their original budgets, even when updated demand suggests reallocations. Resource management research examines how rational decision-makers weigh the benefits of reallocating resources against the costs involved.

*Form (b):*

Yet, due to logistical delays and resource wastage costs, agencies often maintain their original aid plans, even when new signals favor a different distribution. Crisis management studies how rational decision-makers optimize aid distribution under uncertainty.
