- PC 1 (Business Strategy): The description of sunk cost fallacy and decision reversal is factually sound. Executives do face increasing costs when reversing strategic decisions, and market data does accumulate over time. The claim that "earlier decisions may no longer be optimal" is accurate. However, the template is extremely sparse—it lacks the richness and specificity of the Reference's templates, which contain multiple causal mechanisms and feedback loops. - PC 2 (Military Command): The military context correctly describes how tactical decisions become costly to reverse and how intelligence updates strategy. The framing of "battlefield route" as a PATH is reasonable. However, the template's brevity means there are fewer factual claims to verify, and the military instantiation feels generic—it doesn't capture domain-specific military decision-making dynamics (e.g., fog of war, command hierarchy, real-time pressure). Rating: 6

- PC 3 (Personal Life): The personal life context is factually plausible—individuals do face social consequences when reversing life choices, and advice does accumulate. However, the template is so abstract that it's difficult to assess factual precision. The claim that "choice cannot be easily reversed" is vague; some personal choices are reversible, others are not. The context lacks specificity about which life choices are being discussed. Rating: 5

- PC 4 (Scientific Research): The scientific context correctly describes how researchers commit to hypotheses and face resource costs when changing direction. The claim that "experimental data" accumulates is accurate. However, the template conflates two different phenomena: (1) the sunk cost fallacy (irrational persistence), and (2) legitimate scientific commitment to a research direction. The context doesn't clearly distinguish between these. Rating: 6

- PC 5 (Political Campaign): The political context correctly describes how campaigns commit to tactics and face political fallout when reversing course. Polling data does accumulate, and politicians do face reputational costs when changing positions. However, the template is so generic that it could apply to almost any domain involving commitment and reversal costs. Rating: 6

The Reference's "Gradient-Guided Navigation" template, for instance, weaves together sensing, memory, interference, and collective behavior into a coherent narrative. The Target's template is a simple loop: decision → information → reversal cost → stickiness. There is no emergent complexity, no surprising connections between concepts. The parallel contexts are competent but uninspired. Rating: 4

The insight that "reversal costs increase with prior decisions" is not novel; this is a standard observation in the literature on commitment and escalation of commitment. The template does not reveal hidden structural similarities across domains or uncover surprising archetypal patterns. Compared to the Reference's "Gradient-Guided Navigation," which reveals a deep isomorphism between bacterial chemotaxis, mountain climbing, career development, and optimization algorithms, the Target's template is intellectually shallow. It applies a single, familiar concept (sunk cost) across domains without discovering new structural insights. Rating: 4

The Reference's templates are typically 150–200 words and contain multiple causal mechanisms, feedback loops, and nuanced conditions (e.g., "When gradients are steep and consistent...
