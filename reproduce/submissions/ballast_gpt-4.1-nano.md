## Archetype Proposal: The Decision-Making Cycle
### 1. Context-template
A [DECISION] must be made based on [INFORMATION], and once a [DECISION] is taken, it cannot be easily reversed. As [INFORMATION] accumulates, the [DECISION-MAKER] realizes that earlier [DECISION]s may no longer be optimal. [REVERSAL_COST] increases with the number of prior [DECISION]s, often leading the [DECISION-MAKER] to stick with the original [PATH], even when new [INFORMATION] suggests a different course. [DECISION_THEORY] explores how rational [DECISION-MAKER]s weigh the benefits of additional [INFORMATION] against the costs of changing [DECISION].

|--------------------|-------------------|------------------|--------------|---------------------|--------------------|

| INFORMATION        | market data       | intelligence     | advice       | experimental data   | polling            |
| DECISION-MAKER     | executive          | general          | individual   | scientist           | politician         |
| DECISION-MODE      | strategic move     | tactical decision| personal choice | research direction | campaign tactic    |
| REVERSAL_COST      | sunk cost          | risk of failure  | social consequence | resource expenditure | political fallout  |
| PATH               | course of action   | battlefield route| life plan    | research pathway    | campaign route     |
| DECISION_THEORY    | game theory        | military strategy| behavioral economics | scientific methodology | political science  |

### 3. Parallel contexts

**Form (a): Business Strategy**

A business strategy must be made based on market data, and once a plan is taken, it cannot be easily reversed. As market data accumulates, the executive realizes that earlier plans may no longer be optimal. Sunk costs increase with the number of prior plans, often leading the executive to stick with the original course, even when new market data suggests a different approach. Strategic move theory explores how rational executives weigh the benefits of additional market data against the costs of changing course.

**Form (b):**  
A business strategy must be formulated based on market data, and once implemented, it is difficult to reverse. As new market data arrives, the executive recognizes that previous plans may be suboptimal. The sunk cost of previous investments makes changing course costly, so managers often continue with their original strategy even when current data indicates a better alternative. Strategic decision theory studies how rational managers balance the value of additional information against the costs of reversal.

## Archetype Proposal: The Resource Allocation Dilemma
### 1. Context-template
An [AGENT] must allocate [RESOURCE] among competing [TASK], and once a [RESOURCE] is assigned to a [TASK], reallocating it incurs [REVERSAL_COST]. As [INFORMATION] about the effectiveness of each [TASK] becomes available, the [AGENT] updates their [ALLOCATION], but the [REVERSAL_COST] grows with the number of reallocations. Often, the [AGENT] continues to allocate resources to the original [TASK], even when new [INFORMATION] favors a different distribution. [OPTIMIZATION] models how the [AGENT] balances the benefits of reallocating against the costs.

### 3. Parallel contexts

**Form (a): Government Budgeting**

A policymaker must allocate funds among programs, and once a budget is assigned to a program, reallocating it incurs political fallout. As impact assessments of each program become available, the policymaker updates their budget distribution, but the political costs of reallocations grow with each change. Often, the policymaker continues to allocate funds to the original programs, even when new impact data suggests reallocations would be better. Budget optimization models how policymakers balance the benefits of reallocating funds against political costs.

**Form (b):**  
A government official must distribute funds across various programs, and once allocated, reallocations are politically costly. As new impact data on program effectiveness arrives, the official revises the budget, but the political fallout from changing allocations increases with each adjustment. Consequently, officials tend to stick with initial allocations even when updated data indicates a different distribution would be more effective. Optimization models help explain how officials weigh the benefits of reallocating against the political costs.

## Archetype Proposal: The Evolutionary Arms Race
### 1. Context-template
Two [ACTORS] are engaged in a [CONFLICT], where each [ACTOR] must choose between [STRATEGY_A] and [STRATEGY_B]. Once a [STRATEGY] is adopted, switching to the alternative incurs [REVERSAL_COST]. As [INTELLIGENCE] about the opponent's [STRATEGY] increases, each [ACTOR] updates their own [STRATEGY], but the [REVERSAL_COST] grows with the number of switches. Often, both [ACTORS] persist with their initial [STRATEGY], even when new [INTELLIGENCE] suggests a different approach. Game-theoretic models analyze how rational [ACTORS] balance the benefits of adaptation against the costs of switching.

Often, both persist with their initial tactics, even when new intelligence suggests a different approach. Game theory models how rational actors balance adaptation benefits against switching costs.

**Form (b):**  
Two countries are in a military conflict, each choosing between offensive and defensive strategies. Once a strategy is selected, changing it involves resource costs. As intelligence about the opponent's strategy improves, each side updates their own, but the cost of switching rises with each change. Consequently, both tend to stick with their initial strategies, even when new intelligence indicates a different approach would be better. Game-theoretic analysis explains how actors weigh the benefits of adaptation against the costs of switching.

## Archetype Proposal: The Cultural Transmission Process
### 1. Context-template
A [CULTURE] propagates [BELIEF] through a population via [COMMUNICATION], and once a [BELIEF] is adopted by an individual, it is difficult to change due to [REVERSAL_COST]. As [INFORMATION] about alternative [BELIEF]s spreads, individuals update their [BELIEF], but the [REVERSAL_COST] increases with the number of previous [BELIEF] changes. Often, individuals persist with their initial [BELIEF], even when new [INFORMATION] favors a different [BELIEF]. Models of cultural evolution analyze how [COMMUNICATION] and [REVERSAL_COST] influence the stability of [BELIEF].

### 2. Metanym table

| [SLOT]             | Religious Tradition | Scientific Paradigm | Political Ideology | Social Norm | Language Dialect |
|--------------------|---------------------|---------------------|------------------|--------------|------------------|
| CULTURE            | religion            | scientific theory   | political movement | social custom | dialect          |
| BELIEF             | faith               | hypothesis          | ideology         | norm         | pronunciation    |
| COMMUNICATION      | sermons             | publications        | speeches         | social media | conversations    |
| INDIVIDUAL         | believer            | scientist           | voter            | citizen      | speaker          |
| REVERSAL_COST      | cognitive dissonance| reputation damage   | social ostracism| social pressure | language change  |
| INFORMATION        | theological texts   | experimental results| political debates| social cues  | linguistic shifts|
| BELIEF STABILITY   | doctrinal consistency| paradigm robustness | ideological rigidity | norm enforcement | dialect preservation |

### 3. Parallel contexts

**Form (a): Religious Tradition**

A religion propagates faith through sermons, and once a belief is adopted by a believer, it is difficult to change due to cognitive dissonance. As theological texts and interpretations spread, believers update their faith, but the dissonance cost increases with each change. Often, believers persist with their initial faith, even when new theological insights suggest a different belief. Models of religious stability analyze how communication and dissonance costs influence belief persistence.

**Form (b):**  
A religious tradition spreads faith via sermons, and once an individual adopts a belief, changing it involves cognitive dissonance. As new theological texts and interpretations emerge, believers may revise their faith, but the cost of changing beliefs grows with each shift. Consequently, many stick with their original faith even when new interpretations favor a different belief. Theories of religious stability examine how communication and dissonance costs maintain belief consistency.

**Note:**  
These five archetypal contexts span diverse systems: decision cycles, resource management, strategic conflict, cultural evolution, and biological or social systems. They demonstrate different structural features: some recursive (e.g., the decision cycle), some involving strategic interactions, some emphasizing information and costs, and others focusing on propagation and stability.
